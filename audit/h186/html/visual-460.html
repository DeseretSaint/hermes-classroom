# Lesson 1 — two ways of saying it This is a demonstration lesson for an adult beginner (L0). Every Japanese line is followed by romaji and an English meaning. Romaji is a scaffold: it helps you hear the shape of the line, and it is not evidence that you can read Japanese. ## Dialogue A — two friends, after a film Mika: たのしかったね。 (tanoshikatta ne.) — "That was fun, wasn't it?" Jun: うん。また行こう。 (un. mata ikou.) — "Yeah. Let's go again." Mika: うん、好きだよ。 (un, suki da yo.) — "Yeah, I like you." `好き` (suki) is a na-adjective: it describes the person or thing that is liked, not an action the speaker performs. `好きだよ` (suki da yo) is the casual form, used with friends and people you are already close to. The final `よ` (yo) softens the statement and offers it as new information. ## Dialogue B — a confession Aoi: 好きです。付き合ってください。 (suki desu. tsukiatte kudasai.) — "I like you. Please go out with me." This is the shape of a **告白** (kokuhaku), a romantic confession. The polite form `好きです` (suki desu) carries the seriousness of the moment, and `付き合ってください` (tsukiatte kudasai) is the request to start seeing each other. `です` (desu) is the polite copula; it marks that the addressee is being addressed politely (丁寧語, teineigo). It does not by itself make a sentence romantic. The romantic reading comes from the situation and from the request that follows. ## What changes between A and B? | | Dialogue A | Dialogue B | |---|---|---| | Who is speaking | friends | two people not yet a couple | | Setting | casual, after an outing | private, deliberate | | Form | 好きだよ (suki da yo) | 好きです — polite (suki desu) | | What it does | states affection | makes a romantic confession | The words are close; the relationship and the setting decide how the line is heard. A literal translation of English "I love you" is not enough to choose between them. ## Note on the sources The A1 curriculum reference used here teaches `すきです` (suki desu) as a basic sentence pattern, and it presents beginner Japanese with romaji alongside kana. That is a curriculum fact, not a claim that any one phrase is the only natural choice. See `source-notes.md` for every link and for what each source does and does not support.
