# Coursebook engagement brief: genuine engagement vs. padding and dark patterns

**Bottom line.** Mechanics that survive scrutiny carry *information*: what the learner can now do, what changed, what to try next. Mechanics that fail carry *pressure*: loss, comparison, random payout, time-on-task. Information mechanics still work without the reward; pressure mechanics collapse, and hurt the learners who most need help. No-invented-mastery is not a constraint on fun — it is the precondition.

## 1. Evidence

**Self-determination theory.** Motivation quality beats quantity. In a meta-analysis of 344 samples (223,209 students), intrinsic motivation and identified regulation ("I value this") predicted success and persistence; external regulation (rewards, punishment) predicted neither and was linked to lower well-being [1]. SDT classroom interventions raise autonomy, competence and intrinsic motivation (g = 1.14 / 0.48 / 0.58), but heterogeneity is extreme (I² ≈ 98%) [2]. Fuel: autonomy, competence, relatedness. Not points.

**Rewards can subtract.** Across 128 experiments, expected contingent *tangible* rewards undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (engagement-contingent d = −0.40; completion −0.36; performance −0.28), while positive verbal feedback raised it (d = 0.33). Tangible rewards hurt children more than college students [3]. Informational vs. controlling is the distinction: "you got better" supports competence; "do this and get that" moves control outward [4]. Task- and process-level feedback is the strongest informational reward; praise and rewards are the weakest [30].

**Flow is real but incomplete.** Flow — clear goals, immediate feedback, challenge matched to skill — predicts absorption and enjoyment [5], but it is a performance state, not a learning state. Conditions that feel fluent often produce worse long-term retention than effortful ones [6]. Aim for stretch, not perpetual flow: ~80–85% success in adaptive practice, consistent with the "85% rule" for optimal learning [31].

**Goals work; narrow goals backfire.** Specific, difficult goals with feedback beat "do your best" [7], but narrow goals produce tunnel vision, shortcuts, risk-taking and lower intrinsic motivation [8]. Set capabilities ("hold a five-minute conversation about your job"), not farmable numbers.

**Habits are cue-driven, slow, forgiving.** Automaticity approached asymptote over 18–254 days in the best-known field study, and missing one opportunity did not materially affect habit formation [9]. Habits are triggered by context [10]. Attach practice to a stable cue, make the minimum session tiny, make lapses cheap. Implementation intentions (if-then plans for when, where, how) raise goal attainment with d = 0.65 across 94 tests [11].

**Variable rewards are the wrong tool because they work.** Variable-ratio schedules produce high, steady response rates and resistance to extinction — the schedule that keeps slot machines profitable [12]. Random-ratio payouts and near-misses sustain play regardless of learning [13]. That makes stopping hard, not learning likely.

**Loss aversion and streaks.** Losses loom about twice as large as gains (median λ ≈ 2.25) [14]. Streaks exploit that. In the final experiment of a seven-study program, an intact logged streak raised subsequent engagement to 93.1%, a broken streak dropped it to 68.7%, and free repair recovered it to 85.2% [15]. The streak becomes a goal in itself, independent of behavior — so breaking it feels like losing progress that was never learning. That is streak anxiety: practice becomes obligation, and the most engaged learners are the most afraid to stop. Duolingo's data agrees: learners who reach a 7-day streak are 2.4× more likely to return the next day (correlational), and separating streak from daily goal raised Day-14 retention 3.3% relative [16]; Duolingo itself calls loss aversion the lever once streaks get long [17]. Defensible version: consistency windows, grace days, free repair — never zero-reset, paid repair, or "you'll lose 47 days."

**XP, levels, badges.** Gamification has small effects on cognitive (g = 0.49), motivational (g = 0.36) and behavioral (g = 0.25) outcomes; game fiction and social interaction moderate effects [18]. A later meta-analysis found g = 0.257 on intrinsic motivation, a large relatedness effect, and minimal competence effect [19] — the tell: points are not evidence of capability. Levels work as difficulty progression: it increased time and persistence on a desirable task, while player-progression titles mainly improved affect on an undesirable one [20]. Badges work when they certify something real and read as informational; they backfire as attendance trophies [4][21].

**Social comparison is position-dependent.** Leaderboards help learners near the top and reliably hurt those near the bottom. A review of 29 leaderboard interventions recommends absolute boards with low ranks anonymised, ranking on performance and engagement, clear rules, and goal-related feedback [22].

**Dark patterns.** Zagal's taxonomy: *grinding*, *playing by appointment*, *pay to skip*, *monetized rivalries* [23]. A mapping of 87 papers found badges, leaderboards, competitions and points most often reported as causing negative effects — worse performance, motivational issues, gaming the system, cheating [24]. Duolingo fieldwork documents "gamification misuse": fixation on competition and playfulness, distraction from learning, dark nudges, compulsion, herding [25]. For children, a 2026 study documents "exit dark patterns" and a right-to-disconnect problem [26].

## 2. Verdict

| Defensible | Conditional — design-sensitive | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Mastery/evidence maps that move only on evidence | Badges tied to verified capability | Zero-reset streaks; loss-framed reminders; paid repair |
| Choice of goal, session length, difficulty | Levels as difficulty progression, not XP inflation | XP per minute; attendance badges |
| Implementation intentions + cue-based reminders | Small, opt-in leaderboards, bottom ranks hidden | Global public leaderboards; demotion leagues |
| Task/process feedback and error diagnosis | Surprise cosmetic rewards not tied to time | Variable-ratio loot; random XP boosts |
| Spaced retrieval and a review queue | Streaks with grace days and free repair | Grinding; hearts/energy gates; pay-to-skip |
| "You can now…" capability milestones | Narrative/character investment | Inflated progress decoupled from mastery |

## 3. Recommended model: Evidence-backed momentum

1. **Progress is a measurement, not a prize.** Every visible number traces to an evidence state: introduced, practiced, retrieved after delay, transferred. Show coverage ("12 of 18 skills have delayed evidence") and confidence ("strong recall, shaky production"). This expresses the no-invented-mastery policy and is what SDT predicts will support competence [1][4].

2. **Sell the next capability, not the next reward.** "Order a coffee and pay for it" replaces "earn 50 XP." Use goal-gradient framing only where progress is real [27]. Surface small wins weekly — the progress principle is a major driver of inner work life [28].

3. **Make the habit cue-based and cheap.** Onboarding yields an if-then plan ("after breakfast, kitchen table, five minutes"); reminders reference the cue, not guilt [11]. Minimum session: two minutes. Missing a day is a non-event. Track weekly consistency (3 of 7), not a fragile chain; repair is free and automatic [9][15].

4. **Make difficulty the game.** Adaptive challenge, visible stretch, readiness unlocks. Difficulty progression is the level mechanic with evidence [20]. Avoid levels that grow with time spent; they confuse tenure with ability.

5. **Put people in the loop, not on a podium.** Default to personal bests and past-self comparison; opt-in co-op goals, teach-back, parent/child pairs. If competing, use small absolute boards with the bottom anonymised, paired with collaboration [18][22].

6. **Buy fun with fiction, not pressure.** Characters, humor, narrative, cosmetics and delightful feedback are legitimate; game fiction was a significant moderator [18]. They do not lie or punish leaving. Test: if we deleted the reward tomorrow, would this still be worth doing?

**Adults vs. parents and children.** Adults need autonomy and relevance: choose domain, pace, difficulty. Child accounts need stricter defaults: no loss-framed notifications, no public leaderboards, no loot, session limits, and a parent view reporting evidence rather than XP. Children are more susceptible to reward undermining [3] and exit dark patterns [26]; give parents a prep checklist and "what to practice next," not a streak to maintain.

## 4. Metrics and experiments

**North star:** *verified durable learning per active learner per week* — skills/items passing a delayed retrieval (≥7 days) plus at least one transfer item. Pair with **evidence coverage**: share of claimed mastery backed by a passed delayed check. If coverage falls while XP rises, the product is drifting into padding.

**Engagement quality (not volume):** W1/W4 retention; organic return (sessions started without a notification); planned-session completion; time-to-first-real-success; voluntary difficulty increase. Report time-in-app per item mastered, not total time.

**Learning quality:** hint dependence, error recurrence, review-queue adherence, guess rate, delayed vs. immediate performance. Performance during practice is a poor proxy for learning [6].

**Guardrails:** SDT need satisfaction (autonomy, competence, relatedness), a pressure/anxiety item, notification opt-out, churn within 7 days of a lapse, uninstalls after a broken streak, parent complaints, XP-to-evidence ratio, unearned level-ups (target zero). Guardrail regressions are ship-blockers, not trade-offs.

**Experiments:** pre-register; randomize at learner level; run a long-term holdout (1–5% for 90 days) to see durable retention after novelty decays; make delayed learning primary on a subsample and engagement secondary; power for the smallest meaningful learning effect; use CUPED and report confidence intervals [29]. Run a **mechanism-removal test** for every reward: ship to a holdout with the reward stripped and check whether the behavior persists. Pair numbers with diary studies and exit interviews [25]. Beware Goodhart: once XP is the target, it stops measuring anything.

## 5. Explicit anti-patterns

- Zero-reset streaks, loss-framed push notifications ("your 47-day streak dies tonight"), paid streak repair.
- XP for minutes, attendance badges, any expected tangible reward for learning — especially for children.
- Variable-ratio loot, random XP boosts, near-miss animations, "spin to win."
- Global public leaderboards and promotion/demotion leagues.
- Grinding, energy/heart gates, pay-to-skip.
- Progress bars that move without evidence; levels that measure tenure; autoplay and "one more lesson" loops.
